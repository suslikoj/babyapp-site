Cross-reactive allergies in children

In short

A cross-reactive allergy happens when two foods have similar proteins and the immune system mixes them up. With fruit and vegetables, cooking often helps; with nuts, peanuts, seeds or shellfish it doesn’t. A positive test, however, doesn’t mean a real allergy.

Cross-reactive allergies

Why do some foods trigger reactions even when a child is “not allergic” to them?

A cross-reactive allergy occurs when the body reacts to a food that, at first glance, seems unrelated to the original allergen. In reality, however, their proteins have a similar structure—and the immune system simply confuses them.

It is like waving at a stranger on the street because they look like someone you know. The reaction is triggered before you realize the difference.

How does it work?

Every allergen is a protein made up of amino acids. The immune system produces antibodies against certain proteins. If another food has a similar:

  • linear structure (amino acids arranged in sequence), or
  • conformational structure (the three-dimensional shape of the protein),

those antibodies may react to it as well.

And a cross-reactive allergy occurs.

Not all cross-reactive allergies are the same:

Allergens that break down with heat (often lower risk)

This category mainly includes fruits and vegetables. The proteins in these foods have a fragile structure—heat destroys it.

That is why a child may react to raw apple but tolerate baked apple. The same applies to carrot, celery, parsley, and others.

Allergens that are stable and resistant (higher-risk group)

This group includes especially:

  • tree nuts
  • peanuts
  • seeds
  • shellfish

These proteins have a more stable, often linear structure and remain reactive even after cooking. That is why cross-reactions are more common with them.

Cross-reactions within allergen groups:

Common examples include:

  • Tropomyosin – links shrimp, lobster, crab, and other shellfish
  • Parvalbumin – connects most marine and freshwater fish

If a child is allergic to one allergen in the group, they may react to others as well.

Important: A positive test ≠ a true allergy

Cross-reactions often appear in laboratory tests. However, this does not automatically mean the child must avoid the food.

A positive test only shows that the immune system “recognizes” the food. It does not indicate whether it causes:

  • itching
  • swelling
  • hives
  • eczema
  • digestive symptoms

A true allergy is one that causes real clinical symptoms.

Therefore, it is not appropriate to eliminate entire food groups preventively just because they appear in test results. The child would unnecessarily lose important nutrients and dietary variety.

When should cross-reactivity be considered?

To put it simply: Only when a specific food repeatedly triggers a specific symptom.

For example:

  • raw apples cause itching in the mouth
  • but applesauce causes no problem

→ typical pollen–food syndrome

Or:

  • the child is allergic to shrimp
  • and the same symptoms appear after squid or lobster

→ stable cross-reaction within animal-based allergens

What does this mean for you as a parent?

1. Do not eliminate foods based only on test results

Cross-sensitization is common and often without symptoms.

2. Observe real reactions, not theoretical ones

A child may test positive to 10 foods, but in reality react to only one.

3. Heat processing helps with “unstable” allergens

Raw → problem Cooked → often tolerated

4. Heat does not help with “stable” allergens

This includes nuts, peanuts, legumes, seeds, and others.

5. Every child reacts differently

Cross-reactivity is a possibility, not a certainty. There is no need to fear entire food groups in advance.

Quick summary

Cross-reactive allergy results from similarities in protein structure between different allergens. A reaction occurs only when a food truly causes symptoms—not simply because it appears on a test.

That is why it is essential to monitor real clinical symptoms, avoid unnecessary preventive eliminations, and work with the child gradually and safely.

Frequently asked questions

Why does raw apple bother my child but baked apple doesn’t?

Fruit and vegetable proteins are fragile and heat breaks them down. This is typical of pollen–food syndrome – raw apple makes the mouth itch, stewed apple is fine.

Should I remove every food that came up positive in a test?

No. A positive test only means the immune system “recognizes” the food. A real allergy is one that causes symptoms – removing whole food groups as a precaution needlessly costs your child nutrients and variety.

Which cross-reactive allergies are most common?

For example, tropomyosin links shrimp, lobster, crab and other shellfish, and parvalbumin links most sea and freshwater fish.
