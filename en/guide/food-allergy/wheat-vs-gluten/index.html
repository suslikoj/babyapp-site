Wheat vs. gluten: what is your child reacting to?

In short

Wheat is the whole grain, gluten is just one group of its proteins (gliadins and glutenins). A child can therefore be allergic to wheat and tolerate gluten – or the other way round. Wheat allergy in children often fades, while celiac disease is a lifelong autoimmune condition that needs a strict gluten-free diet.

WHEAT vs. GLUTEN

What are you reacting to?

Wheat and gluten are often confused terms. In reality, they are two different things that can cause completely different types of reactions. Understanding the difference helps parents navigate elimination and testing more clearly.

What is WHEAT?

Wheat is a grain. It contains:

  • starches
  • fiber
  • several types of proteins (albumins, globulins, prolamins – including glutenins and gliadins)
  • minerals

A wheat allergy occurs when the immune system reacts to any protein in wheat—not only to gluten.

What can be allergenic in wheat?

  • albumins and globulins
  • gluten proteins
  • enzyme inhibitors (e.g., ATI – alpha-amylase/trypsin inhibitors)

That is why a child may be allergic to wheat but tolerate gluten—or the opposite.

What is GLUTEN?

Gluten is only part of wheat flour—it is a mixture of two main proteins:

  • gliadins
  • glutenins

It creates the elastic structure of dough and is also present in:

  • rye
  • barley
  • certain types of spelt

Gluten is not a separate food—it is just one group of proteins found in wheat.

What are the main differences between wheat allergy and gluten-related reactions?

Wheat allergy

– the immune system reacts to any wheat protein

Reactions may be:

  • skin-related (eczema, hives)
  • digestive (diarrhea, abdominal pain, vomiting)
  • respiratory (runny nose, cough)
  • delayed (eosinophilic inflammation of the digestive tract)
  • anaphylaxis

Important: Wheat allergy is not celiac disease.

A child with wheat allergy may:

  • tolerate gluten-free grains (rice, corn, millet)
  • react to other grains due to protein similarity (barley, rye)

Celiac disease

– an autoimmune condition triggered by a reaction to gliadin (part of gluten)

Course:

  • damage to the intestinal lining
  • malabsorption of nutrients
  • chronic condition

Celiac disease is not an allergy and does not resolve over time.

Non-celiac gluten sensitivity

– non-allergic, non-autoimmune – symptoms similar to celiac disease or intestinal irritation, but without antibodies and without intestinal damage

Can gluten cause an allergic reaction?

Yes. A wheat allergy may also target parts of gluten—but it is still classified as a wheat allergy. Diagnosis and management differ from celiac disease.

Symptoms by reaction type

Wheat allergy (IgE and non-IgE)

  • redness around the mouth
  • eczema
  • hives
  • abdominal pain
  • diarrhea, mucus in stool
  • vomiting
  • nighttime restlessness in infants
  • anaphylaxis

Celiac disease

  • long-term digestive problems
  • poor growth
  • anemia
  • fatigue
  • growth disturbances

Gluten sensitivity

  • bloating
  • diarrhea or constipation
  • abdominal pain
  • headaches, fatigue
  • improvement after removing gluten

How are they managed?

Wheat allergy

→ eliminate wheat as a whole → retest regularly (most children outgrow it by school age) → not a lifelong condition

Celiac disease

→ strict lifelong gluten-free diet → eliminate wheat, barley, rye, spelt

Gluten sensitivity

→ individual approach, not necessarily strict → wheat does not have to be permanently eliminated

Summary – simple explanation

Wheat is the whole grain.

Gluten is only one part of wheat.

A child may be allergic to wheat but not have a problem with gluten.

A child may have celiac disease (reaction to gluten) without having a wheat allergy.

Wheat allergy in children often resolves; celiac disease does not.

Introducing wheat between 4–6 months of age may support tolerance development.

Frequently asked questions

Is wheat allergy the same as celiac disease?

No. Wheat allergy is an immune reaction to any wheat protein and often fades in children. Celiac disease is an autoimmune condition triggered by gliadin (part of gluten); it damages the gut lining and requires a strict lifelong gluten-free diet.

Can a child with wheat allergy eat rye or barley?

A child with wheat allergy often tolerates gluten-free grains (rice, corn, millet), but may also react to barley or rye because their proteins are similar.

What are the signs of wheat allergy in a baby?

For example redness around the mouth, eczema, hives, tummy pain, diarrhea or mucus in the stool, vomiting and restless nights. The most serious reaction is anaphylaxis.

When should wheat be introduced to a baby?

Introducing wheat between 4 and 6 months supports tolerance.
